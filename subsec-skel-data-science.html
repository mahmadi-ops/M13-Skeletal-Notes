    Subsection 3.3.4 Application in Data Science: Measuring Similarity in Tastes

    Every time a streaming service tells you “because you watched Sinners…”, some piece of software has decided that your taste resembles the taste of other people in its database. Deciding whose taste resembles whose can be framed as a geometry problem, and one tool that can help us decide is the dot product.
    🔗
    The first step is to turn taste into a vector. Fix a list of movies and ask each person to rate every one of them on a scale from \(-5\) to \(5\text{,}\) where \(5\) means “loved it”, \(-5\) means “I want those two hours back”, and \(0\) means indifference. Reading the ratings off in the same order for everybody turns each person into a vector, and the question “do these two people have similar taste?” becomes a question about the angle between two vectors, which by Theorem 3.3.3 is exactly what the dot product measures.
    🔗

    Definition 3.3.24. Angular Distance and Cosine Similarity.

    Let \(\mathbf u\) and \(\mathbf v\) be two nonzero vectors. Their angular distance is the angle \(\theta\) between them, obtained from (3.3.2),
    \begin{equation} d_A(\mathbf u,\mathbf v) = \theta = \cos^{-1}\left(\frac{\mathbf u \cdot \mathbf v}{\|\mathbf u\|\|\mathbf v\|}\right).\tag{3.3.8} \end{equation}
    The quantity \(\dfrac{\mathbf u \cdot \mathbf v}{\|\mathbf u\|\|\mathbf v\|} = \cos\theta\) appearing inside is called the cosine similarity of \(\mathbf u\) and \(\mathbf v\text{.}\) The smaller the angular distance, the more similar the two vectors.
    🔗
    🔗
    Angular distance asks “how nearly do these two vectors point the same way?” Notice that it is completely blind to the lengths of \(\mathbf u\) and \(\mathbf v\text{:}\) multiplying either vector by a positive number leaves \(\theta\) untouched. We will come back to what that means for moviegoers.
    🔗

    Two Movies at a Time.

    Two movies give vectors in \(\mathbb R^2\text{,}\) which we can draw. Take two of the ten films nominated for Best Picture at the 2026 Academy Awards, Sinners and Hamnet, and record each person’s rating of Sinners as the first component and of Hamnet as the second. Mehdi, Norm, and Popcorn rate them as follows:
    \begin{equation*} \begin{aligned} \text{Mehdi:} \quad \mathbf u \amp = \langle 4, 5\rangle \\ \text{Norm:} \quad \mathbf v \amp = \langle 5, 3\rangle \\ \text{Popcorn:} \quad \mathbf w \amp = \langle -4, -2\rangle. \end{aligned} \end{equation*}
    Mehdi and Norm both enjoyed the two films; Popcorn sat through both of them and regretted it. Figure 3.3.25 shows the three taste vectors.

    More Movies, More Dimensions.

    Nothing above depended on there being exactly two movies. With \(n\) movies, a person’s taste vector is \(\mathbf u = \langle u_1, u_2, \ldots, u_n\rangle\) in \(\mathbb R^n\text{,}\) the dot product of (3.3.3) becomes a sum of \(n\) products, \(\mathbf u \cdot \mathbf v = \sum_{i=1}^{n} u_i v_i\text{,}\) and the length of a vector becomes \(\|\mathbf u\| = \sqrt{\sum_{i=1}^{n} u_i^2}\text{.}\) The angular distance of Definition 3.3.24 carries over verbatim:
    \begin{equation} d_A(\mathbf u,\mathbf v) = \cos^{-1}\left( \frac{\sum_{i=1}^{n} u_i v_i} {\sqrt{\sum_{i=1}^{n} u_i^2}\,\sqrt{\sum_{i=1}^{n} v_i^2}}\right).\tag{3.3.9} \end{equation}
    🔗
    We can no longer draw the picture, but every formula still applies, and the language of Remark 3.3.6 still means something: two taste vectors with \(\mathbf u \cdot \mathbf v = 0\) are orthogonal, an angular distance of \(90^\circ\text{,}\) which here says that knowing one person’s ratings tells you nothing about the other’s. This is what makes the dot product useful in practice. A streaming service with \(50{,}000\) titles works in \(\mathbb R^{50{,}000}\text{,}\) and finding the users nearest to you is still nothing more than the arithmetic in (3.3.9).
    🔗
