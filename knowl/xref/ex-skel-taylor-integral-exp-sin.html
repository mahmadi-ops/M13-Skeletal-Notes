Example 2.3.19. A non-elementary integral computed exactly.

In Example 2.3.18, term-by-term integration produced a series that we could only estimate. Sometimes, however, the series produced by term-by-term integration is one we already recognize, and then the integral can be evaluated exactly, even though the integrand has no elementary antiderivative. Show that
\begin{equation*} \int_0^{\infty} e^{-x}\,\frac{\sin x}{x}\,dx = \frac{\pi}{4}. \end{equation*}
🔗
Hint. Each power of \(x\) has to be integrated against \(e^{-x}\) on \([0,\infty)\text{.}\) Use the following fact from Calculus II, proved by integration by parts
 1 
Let \(I_k = \int_0^{\infty} x^k e^{-x}\,dx\text{.}\) Then \(I_0 = \int_0^{\infty} e^{-x}\,dx = 1\text{.}\) For \(k \ge 1\text{,}\) integration by parts with \(u = x^k\) and \(dv = e^{-x}\,dx\) gives \(I_k = \Big[-x^k e^{-x}\Big]_0^{\infty} + k\,I_{k-1} = k\,I_{k-1}\text{,}\) since \(x^k e^{-x} \to 0\) as \(x \to \infty\text{.}\) By induction, \(I_k = k(k-1)\cdots 1 \cdot I_0 = k!\text{.}\)
: for every integer \(k \ge 0\text{,}\)
\begin{equation} I_k = \int_0^{\infty} x^k e^{-x}\,dx = k!.\tag{2.3.10} \end{equation}
🔗
Solution.
 2 
Integrating an infinite series term by term over the unbounded interval \([0,\infty)\) needs a justification, and the Remainder Estimation Theorem supplies one. Let \(S_N(x) = \sum_{n=0}^{N} \frac{(-1)^n x^{2n}}{(2n+1)!}\) be the partial sum of the series for \(\frac{\sin x}{x}\text{,}\) a polynomial of degree \(2N\text{.}\) For \(f(x) = \sin x\) every derivative satisfies \(|f^{(k)}(t)| \le 1\text{.}\) The Taylor polynomial of \(\sin x\) of degree \(2N+2\) has no \(x^{2N+2}\) term, so it equals \(x\,S_N(x)\text{,}\) and the Remainder Estimation Theorem gives \(|\sin x - x\,S_N(x)| \le \frac{|x|^{2N+3}}{(2N+3)!}\text{.}\) Dividing by \(x \gt 0\text{,}\) \(\left|\frac{\sin x}{x} - S_N(x)\right| \le \frac{x^{2N+2}}{(2N+3)!}\text{.}\) Multiplying by \(e^{-x}\text{,}\) integrating, and using \(I_k = k!\) with \(k = 2N+2\text{,}\) the integral differs from the partial sum \(\sum_{n=0}^{N}\frac{(-1)^n}{2n+1}\) by at most \(\frac{(2N+2)!}{(2N+3)!} = \frac{1}{2N+3}\text{.}\) This bound tends to \(0\) as \(N \to \infty\text{,}\) so the partial sums of the Leibniz series converge to the integral. Since they also converge to \(\pi/4\text{,}\) the integral equals \(\pi/4\text{.}\) A numerical evaluation of the integral gives \(0.785398\ldots\text{,}\) which agrees with \(\pi/4 = 0.785398\ldots\text{.}\) The bound \(\frac{1}{2N+3}\) also shows why the Leibniz series converges slowly: to guarantee an error below \(10^{-3}\) we need roughly \(500\) terms.
