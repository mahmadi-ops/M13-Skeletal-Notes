    Subsection 4.8.2 Examples: Functions of Two Variables

    Subsubsection 4.8.2.1 Extrema on an Ellipse

    In the first example, we find the greatest and smallest values of a function whose domain is restricted to a curve in the plane, namely an ellipse.
    🔗

    Example 4.8.6. Extrema of \(f(x,y) = xy\) on an Ellipse.

    Find the greatest and smallest values that the function \(f(x,y) = xy\) takes over the ellipse
    \begin{equation} \frac{x^2}{8} + \frac{y^2}{2} = 1.\tag{4.8.7} \end{equation}
    🔗
    Solution. (First rewrite (4.8.7) in the form \(g = 0\) that (4.8.6) requires, then compute both gradients and read the vector equation one component at a time. Substituting one component equation into the other clears \(x\) and leaves an equation in \(\lambda\) alone; it has a second root \(y = 0\text{,}\) which must be discarded — say why. Finish with the constraint, evaluate \(f\) at the points you get, and say what makes the largest and smallest of those values the absolute extrema.)
    🔗
    🔗
    The figure is worth checking against the answer. (Pick the point \((2,1)\text{,}\) compute \(\nabla f\) and \(\nabla g\) there, and confirm that one is a multiple of the other with exactly the \(\lambda\) you found. Then say what the level curves \(xy = \pm 2\) do to the ellipse at the four extremal points, and what the gray level curves \(xy = \pm 1\) do instead.)
    🔗
    🔗

    Subsubsection 4.8.2.2 Absolute Extrema on a Circle

    Example 4.8.8. Absolute Extrema on a Circle.

    Find the absolute maximum and minimum of \(f(x,y) = x^2 + y^2 - 2x + 2y + 5\text{,}\) given the constraint \(x^2 + y^2 = 4\text{.}\)
    🔗
    Completing the squares shows that
    \begin{equation} \begin{aligned} \amp f(x,y) = (x-1)^2 \\ \amp \qquad {} + (y+1)^2 + 3, \end{aligned}\tag{4.8.8} \end{equation}
    so the graph of \(f\) is a paraboloid with vertex at \((1,-1,3)\text{,}\) and the level curves of \(f\) are the circles \((x-1)^2 + (y+1)^2 = c - 3\) centered at \((1,-1)\text{.}\) The constraint \(g(x,y) = x^2 + y^2 - 4 = 0\) is a circle of radius \(2\) centered at the origin. Restricting \(f\) to that circle traces a curve on the paraboloid, and we are after the highest and lowest points of that curve.
    🔗
    Solution. (Compute both gradients and read (4.8.6) component by component. Here adding the two component equations is what unlocks the system: it factors, and one of the two cases it offers is impossible — substitute that value of \(\lambda\) back and say what goes wrong. The other case plus the constraint gives the points; evaluate \(f\) there using (4.8.8), and say why these are the absolute extrema.)
    🔗
    🔗
    There is a second, purely geometric route to the same two numbers. (By (4.8.8), on any point of the plane \(f\) is \(3\) plus the square of the distance to \((1,-1)\text{.}\) Find the points of the constraint circle farthest from and nearest to \((1,-1)\) — the center of that circle is the origin, and \((1,-1)\) sits inside it — and recompute the two extreme values from their distances.)
    🔗
    Note also that in Figure 4.8.10 the gradients \(\nabla f\) and \(\nabla g\) point in the same direction at both extrema, but with different multipliers. (Get each one from the component equation \(x - 1 = \lambda x\text{.}\))
    🔗
    The video below animates this picture. The completed-square form (4.8.8) shows that, with no constraint, \(f\) would take its least possible value \(3\) at the vertex \((1,-1)\)—the bottom of the paraboloid. But that vertex lies inside the constraint circle (a distance \(\sqrt 2 \lt 2\) from the origin), so it is not available to us here: the method of Lagrange multipliers instead locates the highest and lowest points of \(f\) along the circle. As the level circles of \(f\) expand outward from \((1,-1)\text{,}\) the first one to reach the constraint circle gives the constrained minimum and the last gives the maximum; at each of these contacts the two circles are tangent and \(\nabla f\) is parallel to \(\nabla g\text{.}\)
    🔗
    🔗

    Subsubsection 4.8.2.3 Two Applications

    We close the two-variable examples with two problems from outside the classroom: one from a sawmill, and one from the beach that turns out to be a law of optics.
    🔗

    Example 4.8.11. The Strongest Beam Cut from a Log.

    A sawmill cuts a rectangular beam from a round log \(12\) inches in diameter. The strength of a beam, that is, the load it can carry before it breaks in bending, is proportional to its width times the square of its depth:
    \begin{equation*} S = k\,w\,h^2, \end{equation*}
    where the constant \(k \gt 0\) depends only on the wood. What width \(w\) and depth \(h\) give the strongest beam?
    🔗
    Solution. (Write the constraint that ties \(w\) and \(h\) to the diameter of the log, as Figure 4.8.12 suggests, and apply (4.8.6) to \(f(w,h) = wh^2\text{;}\) the constant \(k\) plays no part. Say why the point you find gives the maximum, and compare it with the square beam.)
    🔗
    🔗

    Example 4.8.13. The Lifeguard’s Path and the Law of Refraction.

    A lifeguard at \(A\text{,}\) standing \(a\) meters from the water’s edge, sees a swimmer in trouble at \(B\text{,}\) which is \(b\) meters out from the shore and \(d\) meters down the beach. She runs at speed \(v_1\) on the sand and swims at the slower speed \(v_2\text{.}\) Show that on the fastest route, the angles \(\theta_1\) and \(\theta_2\) that the run and the swim make with the perpendicular to the shoreline satisfy
    \begin{equation*} \frac{\sin\theta_1}{v_1} = \frac{\sin\theta_2}{v_2}. \end{equation*}
    🔗
    Solution. (Let \(x\) and \(y\) be the distances along the shore that she covers while running and while swimming, as in Figure 4.8.14. Write the travel time \(T(x,y)\) and the constraint, apply (4.8.6), and read \(\sin\theta_1\) and \(\sin\theta_2\) off the two right triangles of the figure. Say why a fastest route exists.)
    🔗
    🔗
    Since \(v_2 \lt v_1\text{,}\) the condition forces \(\theta_2 \lt \theta_1\text{:}\) she should not head straight for the swimmer, but run farther along the sand, where she is fast, and shorten the swim. For the numbers of Figure 4.8.14, solving the condition numerically gives \(x \approx 50\) meters. She enters the water only about \(10\) meters short of the point opposite the swimmer and arrives in \(24.8\) seconds, almost \(4\) seconds sooner than along the straight line from \(A\) to \(B\text{.}\)
    🔗
    Light obeys the same rule. Passing from air into water, where it travels more slowly, a ray of light follows the path of least time, and the condition of Example 4.8.13 is Snell’s law of refraction. It is the reason a straw looks bent in a glass of water.
