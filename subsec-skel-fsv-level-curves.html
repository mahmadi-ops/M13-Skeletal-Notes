    Subsection 4.1.2 Level Curves and Contour Maps

    Functions of two variables can be visualized by means of level curves. These are curves that connect points where the function \(z = f(x,y)\) takes on a given value \(c\text{.}\) Take again \(z = f(x,y) = 1 - x^2 - y^2\) and find its level curves. (Set \(f(x,y) = c\) and rearrange until only \(x^2 + y^2\) is on the left; the shape should then be recognizable at a glance. Say what curve you get and what its radius is, and note for which values of \(c\) there is no curve at all.)
    🔗

    Level Curves of \(f(x,y) = 1-x^2-y^2\).

    \begin{equation} 1 - x^2 - y^2 = c \quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad x^2 + y^2 = 1 - c.\tag{4.1.1} \end{equation}
    🔗
    🔗
    Fill in the radius for each of the equally spaced values \(c = \tfrac12,\, 0,\, -\tfrac12,\, -1,\, -\tfrac32\) below, then look at Figure 4.1.3, where these curves are drawn on the surface itself as intersections with the horizontal planes \(z = c\text{.}\) (Notice which way round it goes: larger \(c\) gives the smaller circle. Say why, from the shape of the surface.)
    🔗
    Projecting those level curves down into the \(xy\)-plane gives the contour map (or contour plot) of the function. Draw it on the grid below, labelling each circle with its value of \(c\text{.}\) (Use the same five values, so the contour interval is constant at \(\Delta c = \tfrac12\) — that is what makes the spacing of the circles meaningful. After drawing it, say what close-together contours mean and what far-apart ones mean.)
    🔗

    Example 4.1.4. Sketching Level Curves.

    Sketch some level curves of the function \(g(x,y) = x^2 + \dfrac{y^2}{4}\text{.}\)
    🔗
    Solution. (Set \(g(x,y) = c\text{.}\) For \(c \gt 0\) the result is a conic you met in Section 3.7; name it, and give its two semi-axes in terms of \(c\text{.}\) Then sketch the curves for several values of \(c\) on the grid, and say which direction they are stretched in and by how much. What happens when \(c = 0\text{,}\) and when \(c \lt 0\text{?}\))
    🔗
    The surface \(z = g(x,y)\) itself is shown in Figure 4.1.5; it is the elliptic paraboloid of Subsection 3.8.1. Use it to check the curves you drew.
    🔗
    🔗

    Example 4.1.6. Level Curves of a Saddle.

    Sketch some level curves of the function \(h(x,y) = x^2 - y^2\text{.}\)
    🔗
    Solution. (Set \(h(x,y) = c\text{.}\) This time the shape depends on the sign of \(c\text{:}\) for \(c \ne 0\) it is a conic you met in Section 3.7, so name it and give its vertices, separately for \(c \gt 0\) and \(c \lt 0\text{,}\) and say what \(c = 0\) gives. Then sketch the curves for \(c = -2, -1, 0, 1, 2\) on the grid, and say how the curve for \(c = 0\) is related to all the others.)
    🔗
    The surface \(z = h(x,y)\) itself is shown in Figure 4.1.7; it is the hyperbolic paraboloid of Subsection 3.8.2. Use it to check the curves you drew.
