    Subsection 3.4.5 *Application: Circular Motion: Velocity and Angular Velocity

    Starred section. This one is for the interested reader. It will not be examined.
    🔗
    A second physical application of the cross product describes the motion of an object travelling in a circle: a rider on a circular track, a ball whirled on a string, a point on a spinning wheel, or the moon on its (nearly circular) orbit. In each case the object turns about a fixed centre, and the natural way to keep track of where it is at time \(t\) is not by its coordinates \(x\) and \(y\) but by the single angle \(\theta(t)\) it has swung through. Our goal is to study how the velocity and the rate of change of that angle are related.
    🔗

    Subsubsection 3.4.5.1 A Frame that Turns with the Particle

    Let an object move on a circle of radius \(r\) centred at the origin of the \(xy\)-plane, and let \(\theta(t)\) be the angle its position makes with the positive \(x\)-axis at time \(t\text{,}\) as in Figure 3.4.24. At the point \(P\) where the particle sits we attach two unit vectors:
    • \(\hat r(t)\text{,}\) pointing radially outward, away from the centre, and
      🔗
      🔗
    • \(\hat\theta(t)\text{,}\) perpendicular to \(\hat r(t)\) and tangent to the circle, pointing in the direction of increasing \(\theta\text{.}\)
      🔗
      🔗
    🔗
    Read the components of the moving frame off Figure 3.4.24, and write down the position vector. (\(\hat r\) makes the angle \(\theta\) with the \(x\)-axis, and \(\hat\theta\) is a quarter turn counterclockwise from it. Then check that \(\hat r\text{,}\) \(\hat\theta\text{,}\) \(\hat k\) is right-handed by computing \(\hat r \times \hat\theta\) with (3.4.5); a Pythagorean identity finishes it.)
    🔗

    The Moving Frame.

    \begin{align} \hat r(t) \amp = \cos\theta(t)\,\hat{\imath} + \sin\theta(t)\,\hat{\jmath},\tag{3.4.9}\\ \hat\theta(t) \amp = -\sin\theta(t)\,\hat{\imath} + \cos\theta(t)\,\hat{\jmath},\tag{3.4.10}\\ \overrightarrow{r}(t) \amp = r\,\hat r(t).\tag{3.4.11} \end{align}
    🔗
    🔗
    The pair \(\hat{\imath}\text{,}\) \(\hat{\jmath}\) is fixed once and for all, while the pair \(\hat r(t)\text{,}\) \(\hat\theta(t)\) rides along with the particle and turns as it goes; this is exactly what makes the second pair convenient.
    🔗
    🔗

    Subsubsection 3.4.5.2 The Velocity in Circular Motion

    Because the particle moves, the angle \(\theta\) depends on \(t\text{,}\) and so do the two moving unit vectors. Differentiate the frame, and then the position.
    🔗

    Velocity in Circular Motion.

    \begin{equation} \overrightarrow{v}(t) = r\,\frac{d\theta}{dt}\,\hat\theta(t),\tag{3.4.12} \end{equation}
    whose \(\hat\theta\)-component
    \begin{equation} v_\theta = r\,\frac{d\theta}{dt}\tag{3.4.13} \end{equation}
    is called the tangential component of the velocity. It is positive when the particle turns counterclockwise and negative when it turns clockwise.
    🔗
    🔗

    Definition 3.4.25. Angular Speed.

    The angular speed of the particle is the magnitude of the rate of change of its angle,
    \begin{equation} \omega = \left|\frac{d\theta}{dt}\right|,\tag{3.4.14} \end{equation}
    measured in radians per second. By (3.4.12), the speed of the particle and its angular speed are related by
    \begin{equation} v = \left\|\overrightarrow{v}\right\| = r\,\omega.\tag{3.4.15} \end{equation}
    🔗
    🔗
    It is worth seeing where (3.4.15) comes from geometrically, without any differentiation of unit vectors. Figure 3.4.26 shows the particle moving from \(\overrightarrow{r}(t)\) to \(\overrightarrow{r}(t+\Delta t)\) through a small angle \(\Delta\theta\text{.}\) Recover (3.4.15) from that picture.
    🔗
    🔗

    Subsubsection 3.4.5.3 Angular Velocity as a Vector

    So far the rate of turning has been a number, \(d\theta/dt\text{.}\) But a rotation has a direction as well as a rate: the plane of the circle can be tilted any which way, and the particle can go around it either way. Both pieces of information can be carried by a single vector, provided we point it along the axis of rotation rather than along the motion.
    🔗

    Definition 3.4.27. Angular Velocity Vector.

    For an object moving in a circle in the \(xy\)-plane about the origin, the angular velocity vector is
    \begin{equation} \overrightarrow{\omega} = \frac{d\theta}{dt}\,\hat{k} = \omega_z\,\hat{k},\tag{3.4.16} \end{equation}
    directed along the axis of rotation. Its SI units are \(\text{rad}\cdot\text{s}^{-1}\text{,}\) and the angular speed of (3.4.14) is its magnitude, \(\omega = \left\|\overrightarrow{\omega}\right\| = \left|\omega_z\right|\text{.}\)
    🔗
    🔗
    The sign of \(\omega_z\) records which way the object goes around, and the right-hand rule turns that sign into a direction in space: curl the fingers of your right hand in the direction of the motion and your thumb points along \(\overrightarrow{\omega}\text{.}\) The two cases are shown in Figure 3.4.28.
    🔗
    🔗

    Subsubsection 3.4.5.4 Velocity as a Cross Product

    We now have two vectors attached to the motion: the angular velocity \(\overrightarrow{\omega}\text{,}\) along the axis, and the position \(\overrightarrow{r}\text{,}\) in the plane of the circle. The velocity is perpendicular to both, which is precisely the job of the cross product.
    🔗

    Fact 3.4.29. Velocity and Angular Velocity.

    For an object in circular motion about the origin,
    \begin{equation} \overrightarrow{v} = \overrightarrow{\omega} \times \overrightarrow{r}.\tag{3.4.17} \end{equation}
    🔗
    🔗
    Verify (3.4.17).
    🔗
    The geometry of the cross product delivers all three features of the velocity at once. Fill them in from Figure 3.4.30, with no computation.
    🔗
    There is a bonus. A piece of \(\overrightarrow{r}\) running along the axis is parallel to \(\overrightarrow{\omega}\) and so contributes nothing to the cross product, since \(\hat{k} \times \left(z\,\hat{k}\right) = \overrightarrow{0}\text{.}\) Consequently (3.4.17) remains true when \(\overrightarrow{r}\) is measured from any point on the axis of rotation, not just from the centre of the circle.
    🔗
    🔗
