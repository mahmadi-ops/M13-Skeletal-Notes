    1. Matching Quadric Surfaces.

    Match the equations of the surfaces with the graphs A–F shown above by entering a letter from A to F in each blank.
    🔗
    \(y = 2x^2 + z^2\) matches graph .
    🔗
    \(y^2 = x^2 + 2z^2\) matches graph .
    🔗
    \(x^2 + 2z^2 = 1\) matches graph .
    🔗
    \(y = x^2 - z^2\) matches graph .
    🔗
    Answer 1.
    \(\text{E}\)
    🔗
    🔗
    Answer 2.
    \(\text{B}\)
    🔗
    🔗
    Answer 3.
    \(\text{D}\)
    🔗
    🔗
    Answer 4.
    \(\text{C}\)
    🔗
    🔗
    Solution.
    We identify each surface by slicing, just as in this section. Here the special axis is the \(y\)-axis, so we slice with the planes \(y = c\text{,}\) \(x = c\text{,}\) and \(z = c\text{.}\)
    🔗
    For \(y = 2x^2 + z^2\text{,}\) the plane \(y = c\) gives \(2x^2 + z^2 = c\text{:}\) no trace for \(c \lt 0\text{,}\) a single point when \(c = 0\text{,}\) and ellipses that grow as \(c\) increases. The planes \(x = c\) and \(z = c\) give the parabolas \(y = z^2 + 2c^2\) and \(y = 2x^2 + c^2\text{,}\) both opening in the positive \(y\) direction. Ellipses on one side only together with parabolas: an elliptical paraboloid opening along the positive \(y\)-axis, graph E.
    🔗
    For \(y^2 = x^2 + 2z^2\text{,}\) the plane \(y = c\) gives \(x^2 + 2z^2 = c^2\text{:}\) an ellipse for every \(c \neq 0\text{,}\) on both sides of the origin, shrinking to a single point when \(c = 0\text{.}\) The plane \(z = 0\) gives \(y^2 = x^2\text{,}\) the two crossing lines \(y = \pm x\text{.}\) Ellipses collapsing to a point, together with crossing lines: an elliptic cone along the \(y\)-axis, graph B.
    🔗
    For \(x^2 + 2z^2 = 1\text{,}\) the variable \(y\) is missing, so the plane \(y = c\) gives the same ellipse \(x^2 + 2z^2 = 1\) for every value of \(c\text{.}\) Identical elliptical slices at every station: a cylinder parallel to the \(y\)-axis, graph D.
    🔗
    For \(y = x^2 - z^2\text{,}\) the plane \(z = c\) gives the parabola \(y = x^2 - c^2\) opening in the positive \(y\) direction, while the plane \(x = c\) gives the parabola \(y = c^2 - z^2\) opening in the negative \(y\) direction, and the plane \(y = 0\) gives the crossing lines \(z = \pm x\text{.}\) Parabolas opening in opposite directions: a hyperbolic paraboloid, a saddle, graph C.
    🔗
    Graphs A and F match none of the equations. Slicing the ellipsoid in A gives bounded ellipses in every direction, which none of the equations produce. Slicing the two bowls in F with planes \(y = c\) gives ellipses whose size grows like \(\sqrt{|c|}\text{,}\) unlike the straight-sided linear growth of the cone in B.

    2.

    Identify and sketch the surface corresponding to the following equation:
    \begin{equation*} z^2 - y^2 - x^2 = 0. \end{equation*}
    🔗
    Solution.
    We can rewrite the equation as \(z^2 = x^2 + y^2\text{.}\) Let us look at its traces:
    • \(z = c\text{:}\) the horizontal traces \(x^2 + y^2 = c^2\) are circles of radius \(|c|\) (for \(c \ne 0\)).
      🔗
      🔗
    • \(x = 0\text{:}\) \(z = \pm y\text{,}\) a pair of lines intersecting at the origin.
      🔗
      🔗
    • \(y = 0\text{:}\) \(z = \pm x\text{,}\) a pair of lines intersecting at the origin.
      🔗
      🔗
    The surface is a double cone with its vertex at the origin, opening along the \(z\)-axis. See Figure 5.13.43.

    3.

    Identify and sketch the surface \(x^2 - y^2 + z^2 - 4 = 0\text{.}\)
    🔗
    Solution.
    We can rewrite the equation as \(x^2 + z^2 = y^2 + 4\text{.}\) Let us look at its traces:
    • \(y = c\text{:}\) \(x^2 + z^2 = c^2 + 4\text{,}\) circles with radius \(\sqrt{c^2+4} \ge 2\text{.}\)
      🔗
      🔗
    • \(x = c\text{:}\) \(z^2 - y^2 = 4 - c^2\text{,}\) hyperbolas.
      🔗
      🔗
    • \(z = c\text{:}\) \(x^2 - y^2 = 4 - c^2\text{,}\) hyperbolas (for \(c = 0\text{:}\) \(x^2 - y^2 = 4\)).
      🔗
      🔗
    The surface is a hyperboloid of one sheet whose axis is the \(y\)-axis. See Figure 5.13.44.

    4.

    Identify and sketch the surface corresponding to the equation \(2x^2 - y + z^2 = 0\text{.}\) Give reasons for your choice.
    🔗
    Solution.
    We can rewrite the equation as \(y = 2x^2 + z^2\text{.}\) Let us look at its traces:
    • \(y = 0\text{:}\) \(2x^2 + z^2 = 0\) forces \(x = z = 0\text{,}\) the single point \((0,0,0)\text{.}\)
      🔗
      🔗
    • \(x = 0\text{:}\) \(y = z^2\text{,}\) a parabola in the \(yz\)-plane.
      🔗
      🔗
    • \(z = 0\text{:}\) \(y = 2x^2\text{,}\) a parabola in the \(xy\)-plane.
      🔗
      🔗
    • \(y = c \gt 0\text{:}\) \(2x^2 + z^2 = c\text{,}\) ellipses.
      🔗
      🔗
    The surface is an elliptic paraboloid with vertex at the origin, opening along the positive \(y\)-axis. See Figure 5.13.45.

    5.

    Sketch the surface \(4x^2 - y^2 = -4 - z^2\) in three dimensions. Label any points where the surface crosses the coordinate axes.
    🔗
    Solution.
    Collect everything on the left and divide by \(-4\text{:}\)
    \begin{align*} 4x^2 - y^2 + z^2 \amp = -4\\ -x^2 + \frac{y^2}{4} - \frac{z^2}{4} \amp = 1\\ \frac{y^2}{4} - x^2 - \frac{z^2}{4} \amp = 1 . \end{align*}
    Two minus signs and one plus sign, with the constant \(1\) on the right, identify this as a hyperboloid of two sheets. The axis is the one belonging to the positive term, so the axis is the \(y\)-axis.
    🔗
    The traces confirm this.
    • Setting \(y = k\) gives \(4x^2 + z^2 = k^2-4\text{,}\) which is an ellipse when \(|k| \gt 2\text{,}\) a single point when \(|k| = 2\text{,}\) and empty when \(|k| \lt 2\text{.}\) So there is nothing at all in the slab \(-2 \lt y \lt 2\text{:}\) this is why there are two separate sheets.
      🔗
      🔗
    • Setting \(z = 0\) gives \(y^2/4 - x^2 = 1\text{,}\) a hyperbola opening along the \(y\)-axis, and setting \(x = 0\) gives \(y^2/4 - z^2/4 = 1\text{,}\) likewise.
      🔗
      🔗
    🔗
    Axis crossings. Setting \(y = z = 0\) gives \(4x^2 = -4\text{,}\) impossible; setting \(x = y = 0\) gives \(z^2 = -4\text{,}\) impossible. Setting \(x = z = 0\) gives \(y^2 = 4\text{,}\) so the surface meets the axes only at the two vertices
    \begin{equation*} (0,\ 2,\ 0) \qquad\text{and}\qquad (0,\ -2,\ 0) . \end{equation*}
    See Figure 5.13.46.

    🔗

    6.

    Sketch the domain of \(f(x,y) = \sqrt{y - 3x}\text{.}\)
    🔗
    Solution.
    The square root is defined exactly when its argument is non-negative, so the domain is
    \begin{equation*} \{(x,y) : y - 3x \ge 0\} = \{(x,y) : y \ge 3x\}, \end{equation*}
    the closed half-plane on and above the line \(y = 3x\text{.}\) See Figure 5.13.47.

    7.

    In Figure 5.13.48 are level curves of \(g(x,y) = 2\) and \(g(x,y) = 3\) for some function \(g(x,y)\text{.}\) What’s wrong?
    🔗
    Solution.
    Two level curves belonging to different values can never intersect. At the point \(P\) where the drawn curves cross, the first curve says \(g(P) = 2\) while the second says \(g(P) = 3\text{.}\) Since \(g\) is a function, it assigns exactly one output to the point \(P\text{,}\) so \(2 = 3\) — which is impossible. Hence no function \(g\) can have the level curves shown.

    8.

    Let \(g\) be a function of 3 variables defined by
    \begin{equation*} g(x,y,z) = \sqrt{4x^2 + y^2 + 9z^2}. \end{equation*}
    Find the equation for the level surface of \(g\) through the point \(\left(2, \sqrt{11}, 1\right)\) and sketch this level surface. Label any points where the surface crosses a coordinate axis AND label the value of \(g\) on the surface.
    🔗
    Solution.
    Evaluating \(g\) at the given point,
    \begin{equation*} g\left(2, \sqrt{11}, 1\right) = \sqrt{4(4) + 11 + 9(1)} = \sqrt{36} = 6, \end{equation*}
    so the level surface is \(g(x,y,z) = 6\text{,}\) i.e.
    \begin{align*} \sqrt{4x^2 + y^2 + 9z^2} = 6 \amp \;\Rightarrow\; 4x^2 + y^2 + 9z^2 = 36\\ \amp \;\Rightarrow\; \frac{x^2}{9} + \frac{y^2}{36} + \frac{z^2}{4} = 1. \end{align*}
    This is an ellipsoid with semi-axes \(3\text{,}\) \(6\text{,}\) and \(2\text{.}\) It crosses the axes at \((\pm 3, 0, 0)\text{,}\) \((0, \pm 6, 0)\text{,}\) and \((0, 0, \pm 2)\text{.}\) See Figure 5.13.49.
    🔗
    🔗

    9.

    Sketch the level surface \(f(x,y,z) = z^2 - x^2 - y^2\) for \(f(x,y,z) = 4\text{.}\)
    🔗
    Solution.
    The level surface is \(z^2 - x^2 - y^2 = 4\text{,}\) i.e.
    \begin{equation*} \frac{z^2}{4} - \frac{x^2}{4} - \frac{y^2}{4} = 1. \end{equation*}
    Rewriting as \(z^2 = 4 + x^2 + y^2\) shows that \(z^2 \ge 4\text{,}\) so \(z \ge 2\) or \(z \le -2\text{:}\) no part of the surface lies between the two horizontal planes \(z = \pm 2\text{.}\) The traces are
    • \(z = c\) with \(|c| \gt 2\text{:}\) \(x^2 + y^2 = c^2 - 4\text{,}\) circles;
      🔗
      🔗
    • \(x = c\text{:}\) \(z^2 - y^2 = 4 + c^2\text{,}\) hyperbolas;
      🔗
      🔗
    • \(y = c\text{:}\) \(z^2 - x^2 = 4 + c^2\text{,}\) hyperbolas.
      🔗
      🔗
    Hence the level surface is a hyperboloid of two sheets, symmetric about the \(z\)-axis, with vertices \((0,0,\pm 2)\text{.}\) See Figure 5.13.50.

    10. True or False.

    If \(\lim\limits_{(x,y)\to(a,b)} f(x,y)\) exists, the function \(f(x,y)\) must be defined or continuous at \((a,b)\text{.}\)
    🔗
    Justify your answer fully: give a proof if the statement is true, or a counterexample if it is false.
    🔗
    Answer.
    False.
    🔗
    🔗
    Solution.
    We can for example define \(f(x,y)\) to be zero when \((x,y) \ne (0,0)\) and to be \(1\) when \((x,y) = (0,0)\text{.}\) If we then pick \((a,b) = (0,0)\text{,}\) the limit of \(f(x,y)\) for \((x,y) \to (a,b)\) exists but it is not equal to \(f(a,b)\text{.}\) We could also leave \(f(x,y)\) undefined at the origin to give another counterexample.
    🔗
    🔗

    11. True or False.

    If \(f(x,y) \to L\) as \((x,y) \to (a,b)\) along any line through \((a,b)\text{,}\) then \(\lim\limits_{(x,y)\to(a,b)} f(x,y) = L\text{.}\)
    🔗
    Justify your answer fully: give a proof if the statement is true, or a counterexample if it is false.
    🔗
    Answer.
    False.
    🔗
    🔗
    Solution.
    A first counterexample is the function of Example 4.2.13,
    \begin{equation*} f(x,y) = \frac{(x-1)^2(y-2)^3}{(x-1)^4+(y-2)^6}, \qquad (a,b) = (1,2). \end{equation*}
    Write \(u = x-1\) and \(v = y-2\text{.}\) Along the vertical line \(x = 1\) we have \(u = 0\text{,}\) so \(f(x,y) = 0\text{.}\) Every other line through \((1,2)\) is \(v = mu\text{,}\) and along it
    \begin{equation*} f(x,y) = \frac{m^3 u^5}{u^4 + m^6 u^6} = \frac{m^3 u}{1 + m^6 u^2} \to 0 \quad\text{as } u \to 0. \end{equation*}
    So \(f(x,y) \to 0\) along every line through \((1,2)\text{,}\) and the hypothesis holds with \(L = 0\text{.}\) But along the curve \((x-1)^2 = (y-2)^3\text{,}\) traced as \(x = 1 + t^3\text{,}\) \(y = 2 + t^2\text{,}\) we have \(u = t^3\text{,}\) \(v = t^2\) and
    \begin{equation*} f(x,y) = \frac{t^6\cdot t^6}{t^{12} + t^{12}} = \frac12 \quad\text{for every } t \ne 0, \end{equation*}
    so \(f(x,y) \to \frac12\) along this curve. The two values differ, so \(\lim\limits_{(x,y)\to(1,2)} f(x,y)\) does not exist; in particular it is not \(L = 0\text{.}\)
    🔗
    A second counterexample is
    \begin{equation*} f(x,y) = \begin{cases} 0 \amp \text{if } y \le 0 \text{ or } y \ge 2x^2 \\ 1 \amp \text{if } 0 \lt y \lt 2x^2 \end{cases} \end{equation*}
    Along every straight line passing through the origin this will be constant \(0\) near the origin, but the limit approaching via the parabola \(y = x^2\) is \(1\text{.}\) See Figure 5.13.51.
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    12.

    Discuss continuity of the following functions. At any point where a function is not continuous, say which of the three conditions in Definition 4.2.14 are satisfied there and which are not.
    1. \(\displaystyle f(x,y) = \dfrac{2x^4 y}{x^8 + y^2}\)
      🔗
      🔗
    2. \(\displaystyle g(x,y) = \dfrac{x^2\cos^2 x}{x^2 + (y-1)^2}\)
      🔗
      🔗
    🔗
    Solution.
    1. This is obviously continuous whenever the denominator is nonzero, i.e. everywhere except at the origin. But at the origin it is not continuous (or rather we can’t extend the function to the origin in a way which makes it continuous): if we approach the origin via any of the coordinate axes we get a limit of \(0\text{,}\) but if we approach it via the path \(y = x^4\) we get a limit
      \begin{equation*} \lim_{x\to 0} \frac{2x^4 x^4}{x^8 + x^8} = 1. \end{equation*}
      Hence the limit \(\lim\limits_{(x,y)\to(0,0)} f(x,y)\) does not exist.
      🔗
      In terms of the definition: at every point other than the origin all three conditions hold. At the origin, condition 1 fails, since \(f\) is not defined there (the denominator is \(0\)), and condition 2 fails as well, since the limit does not exist; so condition 3 cannot hold either.
      🔗
      🔗
    2. This is continuous when the denominator is nonzero, so everywhere except at \((0,1)\text{.}\) At \((0,1)\) it can’t be defined in a way which makes the function continuous: approaching \((0,1)\) via the \(y\)-axis, we get the limit
      \begin{equation*} \lim_{y\to 1} \frac{0\cdot\cos^2(0)}{(y-1)^2} = 0. \end{equation*}
      On the other hand, if we approach \((0,1)\) on the line \(y = 1\) we get the limit
      \begin{equation*} \lim_{x\to 0} \frac{x^2\cos^2 x}{x^2} = \cos^2(0) = 1. \end{equation*}
      Hence the limit \(\lim\limits_{(x,y)\to(0,1)} g(x,y)\) does not exist.
      🔗
      In terms of the definition: at every point other than \((0,1)\) all three conditions hold. At \((0,1)\text{,}\) condition 1 fails, since \(g\) is not defined there, and condition 2 fails as well, since the limit does not exist; so condition 3 cannot hold either.
      🔗
      🔗
    🔗
    It is not necessary to discuss whether \(f\) or \(g\) can be made continuous by assigning a value at the point in question: the problem does not ask for it.

    13.

    Evaluate each of the following limits or show that it does not exist.
    1. \(\displaystyle \displaystyle\lim_{(x,y)\to(0,0)} \frac{xy^5}{x^8 + y^{10}}\)
      🔗
      🔗
    2. \(\displaystyle \displaystyle\lim_{(x,y)\to(1,1)} \frac{x^8 - y^8}{x^4 - y^4}\)
      🔗
      🔗
    🔗
    Solution.
    1. Along the path \(x = y^5\text{:}\)
      \begin{equation*} \lim_{(x,y)\to(0,0)} \frac{xy^5}{x^8 + y^{10}} = \lim_{y\to 0} \frac{y^{10}}{y^{40} + y^{10}} = \lim_{y\to 0} \frac{1}{y^{30} + 1} = 1. \end{equation*}
      Along the path \(x = 0\text{:}\)
      \begin{equation*} \lim_{(x,y)\to(0,0)} \frac{xy^5}{x^8 + y^{10}} = \lim_{y\to 0} \frac{0}{0 + y^{10}} = 0. \end{equation*}
      Since \(1 \ne 0\text{,}\) the limit does not exist.
      🔗
      🔗
    2. We factor the numerator as a difference of squares, \(x^8 - y^8 = \left(x^4 - y^4\right)\left(x^4 + y^4\right)\text{,}\) and cancel the common factor \(x^4 - y^4\text{:}\)
      \begin{gather*} \lim_{(x,y)\to(1,1)} \frac{x^8 - y^8}{x^4 - y^4}\\ = \lim_{(x,y)\to(1,1)} \left(x^4 + y^4\right) = 2. \end{gather*}
      🔗
      🔗

    14.

    Find the limit, if it exists, or show that it does not exist:
    \begin{equation*} \lim_{(x,y)\to(1,1)} \frac{x^2 + y^2 - 2}{|x-1| + |y-1|}. \end{equation*}
    🔗
    Solution.
    Along the path \(x = 1\text{,}\) \(y \to 1^{+}\) (so that \(|y - 1| = y - 1\)):
    \begin{equation*} \lim_{y\to 1^{+}} \frac{y^2 - 1}{y - 1} = \lim_{y\to 1^{+}} \frac{(y-1)(y+1)}{y-1} = \lim_{y\to 1^{+}} (y + 1) = 2. \end{equation*}
    Along the path \(x = 1\text{,}\) \(y \to 1^{-}\) (so that \(|y - 1| = -(y - 1)\)):
    \begin{equation*} \lim_{y\to 1^{-}} \frac{y^2 - 1}{-(y - 1)} = \lim_{y\to 1^{-}} -(y + 1) = -2. \end{equation*}
    Since the limits computed along the two paths are not equal, the limit does not exist.

    15.

    Find the following limit, if it exists, or show that it does not exist:
    \begin{equation*} \lim_{(x,y)\to(0,0)} \frac{y^4}{x^4 + 3y^4}. \end{equation*}
    🔗
    Solution.
    Along the path \(x = 0\text{:}\)
    \begin{equation*} \lim_{(x,y)\to(0,0)} \frac{y^4}{x^4 + 3y^4} = \lim_{y\to 0} \frac{y^4}{3y^4} = \frac13. \end{equation*}
    Along the path \(y = x\text{:}\)
    \begin{equation*} \lim_{(x,y)\to(0,0)} \frac{y^4}{x^4 + 3y^4} = \lim_{x\to 0} \frac{x^4}{x^4 + 3x^4} = \frac14. \end{equation*}
    Since \(\frac13 \ne \frac14\text{,}\) the limit does not exist.
