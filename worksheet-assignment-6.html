    These problems intersect lines with planes and measure distances between them, and identify conic sections from their equations. If you would like to review the material first, see Lines and Planes in Space and Conic Sections.

    1.

    Find the vector equation of the line of intersection of the following planes:
    \begin{align*} 2x + y - z \amp = 6,\\ x - y + 3z \amp = 4. \end{align*}
    🔗
    Solution.
    The normal vectors are \(\vec n_1 = \langle 2, 1, -1\rangle\) and \(\vec n_2 = \langle 1, -1, 3\rangle\text{,}\) so the direction vector of the line of intersection is
    \begin{equation*} \mathbf v = \vec n_1 \times \vec n_2 = \begin{vmatrix} \mathbf i \amp \mathbf j \amp \mathbf k \\ 2 \amp 1 \amp -1 \\ 1 \amp -1 \amp 3 \end{vmatrix} = \langle 2, -7, -3\rangle. \end{equation*}
    🔗
    Next we need a point on the line. Setting \(x = 0\) in the equations of the planes gives
    \begin{align*} y - z \amp = 6\\ -y + 3z \amp = 4 \end{align*}
    Adding the two equations gives \(2z = 10\text{,}\) so \(z = 5\) and \(y = 11\text{.}\) Hence the point \((0, 11, 5)\) is on the line, and the vector equation of the line of intersection is
    \begin{equation*} \mathbf r(t) = \langle 0, 11, 5\rangle + t\,\langle 2, -7, -3\rangle. \end{equation*}
    🔗
    🔗

    2.

    Find an equation of a plane containing the line \(\mathbf r = \langle -4, 5, -1\rangle + t\langle -4, 6, -3\rangle\) and parallel to the plane \(-3x - y + 2z = 0\text{,}\) in which the coefficient of \(x\) is \(-3\text{.}\)
    🔗
    Solution.
    Since the desired plane is parallel to \(-3x - y + 2z = 0\text{,}\) it has the same normal vector
    \begin{equation*} \vec n = \langle -3, -1, 2\rangle = \langle A, B, C\rangle. \end{equation*}
    (Note that \(\vec n \cdot \langle -4, 6, -3\rangle = 12 - 6 - 6 = 0\text{,}\) so the line is indeed parallel to such a plane.) When \(t = 0\text{,}\) the point \((-4, 5, -1)\) is on the plane, so from the point-normal form,
    \begin{align*} A(x - x_0) + B(y - y_0) + C(z - z_0) \amp = 0\\ \Rightarrow\quad -3(x+4) - (y-5) + 2(z+1) \amp = 0. \end{align*}
    🔗
    🔗

    3.

    Calculate the distance between the following two lines:
    \begin{align*} L_1: \amp \quad x = 4 - t, \quad y = 3 + 2t, \quad z = -5 + 3t\\ L_2: \amp \quad x = 5 - t, \quad y = 3 + 2t, \quad z = -5 + 3t \end{align*}
    🔗
    Solution.
    We notice that the two lines are parallel (both have direction vector \(\mathbf v = \langle -1, 2, 3\rangle\)) and therefore we can find the distance between a point on \(L_2\) and the line \(L_1\text{.}\) Taking \(S(5, 3, -5)\) on \(L_2\) and \(P(4, 3, -5)\) on \(L_1\text{,}\) we get \(\vec{PS} = \langle 1, 0, 0\rangle\text{,}\) and \(\|\mathbf v\| = \sqrt{1 + 4 + 9} = \sqrt{14}\text{.}\)
    🔗
    Using the cross product,
    \begin{equation*} \vec{PS} \times \mathbf v = \begin{vmatrix} \mathbf i \amp \mathbf j \amp \mathbf k \\ 1 \amp 0 \amp 0 \\ -1 \amp 2 \amp 3 \end{vmatrix} = -3\,\mathbf j + 2\,\mathbf k, \qquad \left\|\vec{PS} \times \mathbf v\right\| = \sqrt{9 + 4} = \sqrt{13}, \end{equation*}
    and the distance is
    \begin{equation*} d = \frac{\left\|\vec{PS} \times \mathbf v\right\|}{\|\mathbf v\|} = \frac{\sqrt{13}}{\sqrt{14}}. \end{equation*}
    🔗
    🔗

    4.

    Find parametric equations for the line through \((2,1,1)\) which is normal to the plane containing \((0,1,1)\text{,}\) \((-1,2,4)\) and \((3,0,1)\text{.}\)
    🔗
    Solution.
    From Worksheet Exercise 5.10.10, the plane through those three points has normal vector \(\vec n = \langle 3, 9, -2\rangle\text{.}\) A line normal to the plane has \(\vec n\) as its direction vector, so through \((2,1,1)\text{,}\)
    \begin{equation*} x = 2 + 3t, \qquad y = 1 + 9t, \qquad z = 1 - 2t, \qquad t \in \R. \end{equation*}

    5.

    Let \(L\) be the line given by
    \begin{equation*} x = 4+t, \qquad y = -1-2t, \qquad z = 5+t . \end{equation*}
    Find the point on \(L\) that is closest to \((-2,2,-1)\text{.}\)
    🔗
    Solution.
    Write \(Q = (-2,2,-1)\) and let \(P(t) = (4+t,\ -1-2t,\ 5+t)\) be the general point of \(L\text{.}\) The direction vector of the line is \(\vec d = \left\langle 1,\ -2,\ 1\right\rangle\text{.}\)
    🔗
    The closest point is the foot of the perpendicular from \(Q\text{,}\) so we want the value of \(t\) for which \(\vec{QP}\) is orthogonal to \(\vec d\text{.}\) First,
    \begin{align*} \vec{QP} \amp = \left\langle (4+t)-(-2),\ (-1-2t)-2,\ (5+t)-(-1) \right\rangle\\ \amp = \left\langle 6+t,\ -3-2t,\ 6+t \right\rangle . \end{align*}
    🔗
    Now impose \(\vec{QP}\cdot\vec d = 0\text{:}\)
    \begin{align*} (6+t)(1) + (-3-2t)(-2) + (6+t)(1) \amp = 0\\ 6 + t + 6 + 4t + 6 + t \amp = 0\\ 18 + 6t \amp = 0\\ t \amp = -3 . \end{align*}
    🔗
    Substituting \(t = -3\) into the parametric equations gives the closest point
    \begin{equation*} P(-3) = \left(4-3,\ -1+6,\ 5-3\right) = (1,\ 5,\ 2) . \end{equation*}
    As a check, at \(t=-3\) we get \(\vec{QP} = \left\langle 3,\ 3,\ 3 \right\rangle\text{,}\) and
    \begin{equation*} \vec{QP}\cdot\vec d = \left\langle 3,3,3\right\rangle\cdot\left\langle 1,-2,1\right\rangle = 3 - 6 + 3 = 0 , \end{equation*}
    as required. The distance from \(Q\) to the line is \(\|\vec{QP}\| = \sqrt{9+9+9} = 3\sqrt3\text{.}\)
    🔗
    🔗

    6.

    A constant force is
    \begin{equation*} \mathbf F = \left\langle 2, -1, 3 \right\rangle . \end{equation*}
    A particle is constrained to move in directions parallel to the plane
    \begin{equation*} 2x - y + 2z = 0 . \end{equation*}
    For a displacement of one unit, the work done by the force is \(W = \mathbf F\cdot\mathbf d\text{,}\) where \(\mathbf d\) is an allowed unit direction lying in the plane.
    1. Find the unit direction in the plane that maximizes the work.
      🔗
      🔗
    2. Find the maximum possible work per unit distance.
      🔗
      🔗
    3. Find one unit direction in the plane for which the work is zero.
      🔗
      🔗
    🔗
    Solution.
    Reading the coefficients of the plane, a normal vector is
    \begin{equation*} \mathbf n = \left\langle 2, -1, 2 \right\rangle , \qquad \|\mathbf n\| = \sqrt{4+1+4} = 3 . \end{equation*}
    A vector is parallel to the plane exactly when it is orthogonal to \(\mathbf n\text{.}\)
    🔗
    (a) Decompose the force into a normal piece and a piece lying in the plane,
    \begin{equation*} \mathbf F = \operatorname{proj}_{\mathbf n}\mathbf F + \mathbf F_{\parallel} . \end{equation*}
    The first piece comes from the projection formula. Since
    \begin{align*} \mathbf F\cdot\mathbf n \amp = \left\langle 2,-1,3\right\rangle\cdot\left\langle 2,-1,2\right\rangle = 2(2) + (-1)(-1) + 3(2)\\ \amp = 4 + 1 + 6 = 11 , \end{align*}
    and \(\|\mathbf n\|^2 = 9\text{,}\) we get
    \begin{equation*} \operatorname{proj}_{\mathbf n}\mathbf F = \frac{11}{9}\left\langle 2,-1,2\right\rangle = \left\langle \frac{22}{9},\ -\frac{11}{9},\ \frac{22}{9}\right\rangle . \end{equation*}
    Subtracting,
    \begin{align*} \mathbf F_{\parallel} \amp = \mathbf F - \operatorname{proj}_{\mathbf n}\mathbf F = \left\langle 2,-1,3\right\rangle - \left\langle \frac{22}{9},\ -\frac{11}{9},\ \frac{22}{9}\right\rangle\\ \amp = \left\langle -\frac49,\ \frac29,\ \frac59\right\rangle = \frac19\left\langle -4,\ 2,\ 5 \right\rangle . \end{align*}
    As a check, this really is parallel to the plane: \(\left\langle -4,2,5\right\rangle\cdot\left\langle 2,-1,2\right\rangle = -8 - 2 + 10 = 0\text{.}\)
    🔗
    Now let \(\mathbf d\) be any allowed unit direction. Because \(\operatorname{proj}_{\mathbf n}\mathbf F\) is orthogonal to every vector lying in the plane,
    \begin{equation*} W = \mathbf F\cdot\mathbf d = \left(\operatorname{proj}_{\mathbf n}\mathbf F + \mathbf F_{\parallel}\right)\cdot\mathbf d = \mathbf F_{\parallel}\cdot\mathbf d . \end{equation*}
    The normal part of the force is simply wasted: it presses the particle against the constraint and does no work. Writing \(\mathbf F_{\parallel}\cdot\mathbf d = \|\mathbf F_{\parallel}\|\,\|\mathbf d\|\cos\theta = \|\mathbf F_{\parallel}\|\cos\theta\text{,}\) the largest value occurs when \(\cos\theta = 1\text{,}\) that is when \(\mathbf d\) points in the same direction as \(\mathbf F_{\parallel}\text{.}\) Since
    \begin{equation*} \left\|\left\langle -4,2,5\right\rangle\right\| = \sqrt{16+4+25} = \sqrt{45} = 3\sqrt5 , \end{equation*}
    the maximizing unit vector is
    \begin{equation*} \mathbf d_{\max} = \frac{\left\langle -4,\ 2,\ 5\right\rangle}{3\sqrt5} . \end{equation*}
    See Figure 5.9.14.
    🔗
    (b) The maximum work per unit displacement is the length of the in-plane component,
    \begin{align*} W_{\max} \amp = \left\|\mathbf F_{\parallel}\right\| = \left|\frac19\left\langle -4,2,5\right\rangle\right|\\ \amp = \frac19\left(3\sqrt5\right) = \frac{\sqrt5}{3} \approx 0.745 . \end{align*}
    We can confirm this directly from the original force:
    \begin{align*} \mathbf F\cdot\mathbf d_{\max} \amp = \left\langle 2,-1,3\right\rangle\cdot \frac{\left\langle -4,2,5\right\rangle}{3\sqrt5} = \frac{-8-2+15}{3\sqrt5}\\ \amp = \frac{5}{3\sqrt5} = \frac{\sqrt5}{3} . \end{align*}
    Compare this with \(\|\mathbf F\| = \sqrt{14} \approx 3.74\text{:}\) the constraint costs us about \(80\%\) of the force.
    🔗
    The animation in Figure 5.9.15 runs through parts (a) and (b) together.
    🔗
    (c) We want a unit vector \(\mathbf d = \left\langle x,y,z\right\rangle\) satisfying two conditions at once: it must lie in the plane, and it must do no work. That is,
    \begin{equation*} \mathbf d\cdot\mathbf n = 0 \qquad\text{and}\qquad \mathbf d\cdot\mathbf F = 0 , \end{equation*}
    or explicitly
    \begin{align*} 2x - y + 2z \amp = 0 ,\\ 2x - y + 3z \amp = 0 . \end{align*}
    Subtracting the first equation from the second gives \(z = 0\text{,}\) and then \(2x - y = 0\text{,}\) so \(y = 2x\text{.}\) Taking \(x = 1\) gives the non-zero vector \(\left\langle 1,2,0\right\rangle\text{,}\) whose length is \(\sqrt{1+4} = \sqrt5\text{.}\) Hence one suitable unit vector is
    \begin{equation*} \mathbf d_0 = \frac{1}{\sqrt5}\left\langle 1,\ 2,\ 0\right\rangle . \end{equation*}
    Indeed \(\mathbf F\cdot\mathbf d_0 = \tfrac{1}{\sqrt5}(2 - 2 + 0) = 0\text{,}\) and the opposite direction \(-\mathbf d_0\) works equally well. Geometrically, \(\mathbf d_0\) is the direction in the plane perpendicular to \(\mathbf F_{\parallel}\text{:}\) moving along it, the particle travels neither with nor against the usable part of the force.

    7.

    Find the equation of the hyperbola with asymptotes \(y = 3x\) and \(y = -3x\) passing through the point \((0,2)\text{.}\)
    🔗
    Solution.
    Since the hyperbola meets the \(y\)-axis (at \((0,2)\)) it opens up and down, so it has the form
    \begin{equation*} \frac{y^2}{a^2} - \frac{x^2}{b^2} = 1, \end{equation*}
    whose asymptotes are \(y = \pm\dfrac{a}{b}x\text{.}\) Setting \(x = 0\text{,}\) \(y = 2\) gives \(a = 2\text{,}\) and matching the asymptote slopes,
    \begin{equation*} \frac{a}{b} = 3 \;\Rightarrow\; b = \frac{a}{3} = \frac23. \end{equation*}
    Therefore
    \begin{equation*} \frac{y^2}{4} - \frac{x^2}{\frac49} = 1 \;\Rightarrow\; \frac{y^2}{4} - \frac{9x^2}{4} = 1 \;\Rightarrow\; y^2 - 9x^2 = 4. \end{equation*}
    See Figure 5.9.16.

    8.

    Find the equation of the parabola with vertex at the origin and axis of symmetry the \(y\)-axis, passing through the point \((4,2)\text{.}\) Then find its focus and its directrix.
    🔗
    Solution.
    The vertex is at the origin and the axis of symmetry is the \(y\)-axis, so the parabola has the form
    \begin{equation*} x^2 = 4py, \end{equation*}
    whose focus is \((0,p)\) and whose directrix is the line \(y = -p\text{.}\) Only the one number \(p\) is unknown, and the point \((4,2)\) pins it down:
    \begin{equation*} 4^2 = 4p(2) \;\Rightarrow\; 16 = 8p \;\Rightarrow\; p = 2. \end{equation*}
    Therefore
    \begin{equation*} x^2 = 8y, \qquad\text{that is}\qquad y = \frac{x^2}{8}. \end{equation*}
    Since \(p = 2 \gt 0\) the parabola opens upward, its focus is \((0,2)\) and its directrix is the horizontal line \(y = -2\text{.}\)
    🔗
    As a check on the defining property of a parabola, the point \((4,2)\) is at distance \(\sqrt{(4-0)^2 + (2-2)^2} = 4\) from the focus and at distance \(2 - (-2) = 4\) from the directrix, and the two agree. See Figure 5.9.17.

    9.

    Two trees grow 100 feet apart. A 120 foot rope is attached to the first tree at a height of 100 feet and to the second tree at a height of 50 feet. A child folds an empty plastic water bottle over the rope and rappels from the high end of the rope to the low end. At each moment, the rope is taut. The point where the water bottle is folded over the rope traces a path. Ignoring the numbers, state what kind of curve the path makes: line, ellipse, parabola or hyperbola.
    🔗
    Solution.
    Let \(A\) and \(B\) be the two attachment points and \(P\) the point where the bottle folds over the taut rope. Because the rope is taut and has fixed total length, the two straight segments \(\overline{AP}\) and \(\overline{PB}\) together always use up the whole rope:
    \begin{equation*} |AP| + |PB| = 120 \text{ feet} \qquad\text{(constant).} \end{equation*}
    This is precisely the defining property of an ellipse with foci \(A\) and \(B\text{.}\) (The curve exists because \(120 \gt |AB|\text{:}\) the distance between the attachment points is \(\sqrt{100^2 + 50^2} \approx 111.8 \lt 120\) feet.) So the path is an arc of an ellipse, lying in the vertical plane containing the two trees. Since the bottle hangs below the rope, \(P\) stays on the lower half of the ellipse. See Figure 5.9.18 and Figure 5.9.19.
